# Jane Eyre — whole-book summary

## Synopsis

Charlotte Brontë's *Jane Eyre* follows an orphan who wants love and belonging without surrendering conscience, freedom, or self-respect. Abused by the Reed family and publicly humiliated at Lowood School, Jane learns both resistance and self-command. As governess at Thornfield Hall, she meets Edward Rochester, whose force of mind answers her own. They fall in love across differences of wealth, age, and rank, but Rochester has concealed a living wife, Bertha Mason, in the house. When their wedding is stopped, he asks Jane to live with him outside the law. She leaves because accepting would make her dependent on his private moral judgment and would destroy the self he claims to love.

Nearly starving, Jane is rescued by the Rivers siblings. She becomes a village schoolmistress, discovers that the Riverses are her cousins, and inherits a fortune that she immediately shares with them. Work, money, and family give her the practical independence that moral resolution alone could not. She then resists an opposite danger: St. John Rivers asks her to marry without love and become an instrument of his missionary ambition. A mysterious cry from Rochester recalls her. Jane returns freely and finds Thornfield burned, Bertha dead, and Rochester blinded and maimed after trying to save everyone. Because Jane now has a home, kin, wealth, and alternatives—and Rochester has confronted consequence and dependence—their renewed marriage can become chosen companionship rather than possession. Ten years later, Jane describes a union of mutual confidence and service without servitude.

## Narrative summary

### Gateshead and Lowood

Jane Eyre is an orphan living on sufferance with her aunt, Mrs. Reed, and the Reed children at Gateshead. She is excluded from family intimacy, assaulted by her cousin John, and punished for defending herself. Confinement in the red-room, where her dead uncle once lay, brings terror and collapse, but it also concentrates her knowledge that the household's account of her character is false. The apothecary Mr. Lloyd recognizes her unhappiness and recommends school. Before leaving, Jane openly confronts Mrs. Reed's cruelty and rejects the lie that she is deceitful.

Lowood School initially enlarges the same pattern into an institution. Under Mr. Brocklehurst, pupils are cold, hungry, and shamed in the name of Christian humility while his own family displays comfort and finery. He publicly labels Jane a liar. Two people prevent the experience from reducing her either to submission or revenge. Her friend Helen Burns treats suffering within a spiritual horizon of forgiveness and life beyond death; the superintendent Miss Temple listens, investigates, and has Jane's name cleared. A typhus epidemic exposes Lowood's neglect and forces reform, while Helen dies of consumption in Jane's arms. Jane remains for eight years, first as pupil and then teacher. When Miss Temple marries and leaves, Jane realizes that tranquility has become confinement. She advertises for a new position and goes to Thornfield.

### Thornfield: recognition and concealment

At Thornfield Jane teaches Adèle Varens and is treated kindly by Mrs. Fairfax, but an eerie laugh from the third storey signals a hidden life. Jane first meets Rochester on the road without knowing he owns the estate; she helps him after his horse falls, establishing a relationship in action before rank can define it. In conversation Rochester questions and commands, but Jane refuses flattery and answers as one mind to another. He is fascinated by her drawings, judgment, and independence. He explains that Adèle may be the daughter of his former mistress, Céline Varens.

One night Jane saves Rochester from a fire in his bed. He blames the servant Grace Poole and demands secrecy, though Grace's freedom and composure make the explanation doubtful. Rochester then brings home an aristocratic party dominated by the beautiful Blanche Ingram. Jane hears Blanche ridicule governesses and sees Rochester appear to court her. Forced to acknowledge her love, Jane nevertheless tries to distinguish feeling from entitlement. Blanche has rank and beauty but little kindness or originality, and Rochester watches her too knowingly to seem in love.

The house party turns courtship into theater. Rochester and Blanche perform a mock wedding in charades; a supposed gypsy fortune-teller privately disillusions Blanche and probes Jane's guarded heart. The gypsy is Rochester in disguise. His control vanishes when Jane reports the arrival of Richard Mason from Jamaica. That night a woman in the hidden rooms stabs and bites Mason. Jane tends him in silence while Rochester fetches a surgeon, and Mason is removed before dawn. Rochester then gives Jane a hypothetical account of a grave past error and a beloved person who might regenerate him. Jane refuses the premise that salvation can rest on another human being. She tells him she will obey only in what she believes right.

### Proposal, revelation, and flight

Jane is recalled to Gateshead after John Reed's ruin and probable suicide precipitate Mrs. Reed's final illness. She returns able to forgive the aunt who still cannot love her. Mrs. Reed confesses a further betrayal: Jane's uncle John Eyre wrote from Madeira intending to adopt her and leave her his property, but Mrs. Reed replied that Jane had died at Lowood. Jane offers forgiveness; Mrs. Reed dies unreconciled.

On returning to Thornfield, Jane and Rochester can no longer hide how much her absence mattered. In the orchard he pretends that marriage to Blanche will send Jane to Ireland. Grief drives Jane to declare that poverty, plainness, and dependence do not make her an automaton: her spirit addresses his spirit as an equal, and she is a free human being with an independent will. Rochester reveals that he does not love Blanche and proposes to Jane. She accepts, but the scene contains warnings. He speaks of atonement and defies judgment; lightning splits the chestnut tree beneath which they became engaged.

During their courtship Jane defends the equality she has asserted. She rejects jewels and an aristocratic bridal identity, refuses to become Rochester's angel or possession, condemns his manipulation of Blanche, and insists on continuing to earn wages before marriage. Rochester loves her resistance yet withholds the full confidence she asks for. On the wedding eve, after dreams of separation and ruined Thornfield, Jane wakes to see a terrifying woman put on her veil, tear it in two, and trample it. Rochester again names Grace Poole and delays explanation.

At the altar, solicitor Briggs announces that Rochester already has a living wife. Mason proves that Rochester married his sister Bertha in Jamaica fifteen years earlier. Rochester admits his intention to commit bigamy and takes the wedding party upstairs, where Bertha is confined under Grace Poole's care. The revelation unites the laugh, the bedroom fire, Mason's wounds, and the torn veil. John Eyre, having learned the truth through Mason, arranged the intervention.

Rochester tells Jane that his father and brother engineered the Jamaican marriage for money while concealing illness in Bertha's family. He describes Bertha's deterioration, her confinement, and his subsequent years of wandering and mistresses. His account explains his misery but not his deception of Jane. He asks her to live with him in France as his true partner. Jane loves him and fears what abandonment may do to him, yet recognizes that becoming his mistress would eventually corrupt both his regard and her self-respect. Principles matter precisely when temptation is strongest. After a dream commands her to flee, she leaves Thornfield before dawn with little money and no destination.

### Moor House: the construction of independence

Jane's coach leaves her at Whitcross, where the accidental loss of her parcel makes her absolutely destitute. She sleeps on the moor, seeks any honest employment, begs for food, and discovers how quickly a person without money, references, or recognized identity becomes socially invisible. Near death, she reaches Moor House. The housekeeper Hannah distrusts the apparent vagrant, but St. John Rivers admits her for examination, and his sisters Diana and Mary respond with immediate compassion. Jane protects herself under the name Jane Elliott.

The Rivers sisters give Jane the intellectual companionship and easy affection she has never possessed. St. John, a reserved clergyman, finds her a cottage and work teaching poor girls at Morton. Jane initially recoils from her pupils' poverty and ignorance, then corrects her prejudice by discovering their distinct intelligence, character, and capacity. The school gives her useful labor, local affection, and economic independence. She confirms that this honest freedom is better than luxurious bondage with Rochester, though she still dreams of him and grieves.

St. John plans missionary work in India. His apparent serenity conceals enormous ambition and a habit of mastering feeling. He passionately loves the beautiful Rosamond Oliver and could marry her with her father's approval, but rejects her because she would not fit his missionary design. When he finds the name “Jane Eyre” written on a scrap from one of Jane's drawings, he connects her to a letter from Briggs. John Eyre has died and left Jane twenty thousand pounds. St. John, Diana, and Mary are also Jane's cousins: their mother was the sister of Jane's father and John Eyre. Jane values the discovered family more than the money and divides the inheritance equally among the four cousins. Wealth becomes a means of justice, reunion, and a shared home rather than status.

St. John then subjects Jane to a second test of selfhood. He trains her in Hindustani and asks her to marry him as a missionary helpmeet. He admires her discipline, courage, and capacity for sacrifice but does not love her; he wants a colleague he can influence and retain absolutely. Jane is willing to contemplate the work and even an early death, but offers to go only as his sister. He insists that refusal of his marriage plan is refusal of God's call. His scriptural authority and moral intensity nearly persuade Jane to merge her will into his. When she asks for certainty, she hears Rochester's voice cry her name from an impossible distance. She answers that she is coming, breaks St. John's ascendancy, and resolves to learn Rochester's fate herself.

### Return and marriage

Jane travels back with money, family, and a home behind her—conditions radically different from those of her flight. She finds Thornfield a blackened ruin, as her dream predicted. Bertha escaped, set the house on fire, and jumped from the roof as Rochester approached her. Rochester saved the servants and tried to save Bertha, but the collapse blinded him and cost him one hand. He has withdrawn to Ferndean.

At Ferndean Jane first restores herself to Rochester through voice and touch. She tells him that she is rich, has family, and is her own mistress. She offers to stay because she chooses him, not because she has nowhere else to go. Her teasing account of St. John revives Rochester from despair and clarifies that neither pity nor another attachment governs her return. He proposes again. Jane accepts a union now lawful, informed, and materially free. Rochester has also changed: disability has made him able to receive care, and he acknowledges pride, wrongdoing, repentance, and dependence on mercy. He describes crying Jane's name at the exact hour she heard him and hearing her answer across the distance.

Jane's famous announcement—“Reader, I married him”—places agency with the narrator. Ten years later she describes marriage not as her absorption into Rochester's life but as continuous conversation and mutual confidence, a closeness as free as solitude. She gladly serves as his eyes and hand because the service is chosen and received without humiliation. Rochester later regains partial sight and sees their firstborn son. Jane ensures that Adèle receives a kinder education; Diana and Mary marry men they love and remain close to her. St. John never marries and pursues his missionary vocation in India to an early death. The novel closes with his expectation of heaven, preserving the grandeur of his faith while Jane's own life affirms a different vocation: earthly love made ethical by freedom.

## Principal character arcs

- **Jane Eyre:** Her governing problem is not simply how to obtain love, but how to remain a full moral person while loving. She progresses from resistance without power, through education and conscience, to practical independence in work, money, and family. Only then can intimacy become a free act rather than submission.

- **Edward Rochester:** Rochester recognizes Jane's mind but initially tries to control the conditions of her choice through secrecy, jealousy, wealth, and private moral exemption. Exposure, loss, attempted rescue, disability, and repentance make him capable of receiving care and asking for a union he can no longer engineer.

- **St. John Rivers:** St. John is Rochester's structural opposite. Rochester tempts Jane to sacrifice principle to passion; St. John tempts her to sacrifice feeling and identity to duty. His courage and faith are real, but his vocation turns other people—including himself—into instruments of an absolute design.

- **Helen Burns:** Helen gives Jane a language of forgiveness, spiritual dignity, and hope beyond retaliation. Jane never adopts Helen's complete submission to earthly injustice, but her mature mercy toward Mrs. Reed depends on what Helen taught her.

- **Bertha Mason Rochester:** Bertha is first presented as sound, fire, and violence hidden inside the domestic structure, then revealed as the living person whose existence invalidates Rochester's story of freedom. The narrative gives her little voice outside Rochester's hostile account, but her confinement is the fact that makes Jane's informed consent impossible and her destruction of Thornfield ends the old order.

## Central themes

- **Freedom must be practical as well as spiritual.** Jane's inner resistance matters, but the Moor House arc supplies the money, work, kinship, and alternatives required to make consent materially real.

- **Love requires equality without sameness.** Jane does not seek equal wealth, appearance, or temperament. She demands recognition that her conscience, feeling, and will have the same human status as Rochester's.

- **Conscience mediates between passion and duty.** Rochester's passion and St. John's duty each become destructive when made absolute. Jane's path accepts both feeling and obligation while refusing either man's claim to define God's will for her.

- **Home is a relationship that preserves movement.** Gateshead, Lowood, Thornfield, and Moor House each test whether belonging confines or enlarges Jane. Ferndean becomes home only after she can leave it.

- **Fire and ice mark rival dangers.** Thornfield's fire expresses passion, secrecy, violence, and transformation; St. John's coldness expresses disciplined repression. Jane needs warmth without conflagration and principle without emotional death.
